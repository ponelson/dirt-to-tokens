A data center is a power contract with a building around it.

Power comes in, heat goes out, and nearly everything in between is equipment for doing both without interruption. The delivery models that fund these buildings differ mainly in how far up that chain a provider builds before it hands over the keys. In the benchmarks cited here, each step up generally means more capital per megawatt, more gross revenue per kilowatt, and assets that age faster, though individual deals move items across the line.

01 · The map

Ten levels, from dirt to tokens

Every deal in this note is defined by which of these levels one party supplies and which the other brings.

It helps to read a data center from the ground up. At the bottom is land with the right to build on it. Above that is the right to draw power from the grid, which CBRE describes as the most decisive factor in site selection, leasing and pricing in 2026.1 Then comes the building itself, the electrical plant that conditions and protects the power, the mechanical plant that removes heat, the fit-out inside the halls, the network that connects the building to the world, the computing hardware, the people who run the facility around the clock, and finally the software that turns hardware into something a customer can buy by the hour or by the token.

The building is the least interesting part of the building. One 2026 construction benchmark puts the structure at roughly 10 to 15 percent of construction cost, with electrical infrastructure the single largest share.8 The concrete matters for schedule and for loads; most of the money and risk sit in power and cooling, and then, in AI builds, in the servers.45

03 · How the money gets made

Sixty years of selling the same three things

Data center companies have always sold some mix of space and power, connections, and computing. What changed is which one carried the margin and who paid for the building.

Before there was a data center industry, computers lived in rooms their owners built, and outside firms sold time on machines they owned.i The business as a sector of its own grew out of the commercial internet. Equinix was founded on 22 June 1998 around one idea, a neutral exchange where competing networks could interconnect, and opened with $12 million and half a building in Ashburn, Virginia, the seed of what became Data Center Alley.146 CoreSite began in 2001 inside One Wilshire, a Los Angeles carrier hotel where networks met to swap traffic.147

04 · Levels 1 and 2

Much of a site's value is its power position

Land near a substation with a signed utility commitment trades as a different asset from the same acreage without one.

Site selection used to start with fiber, latency and tax treatment. It now usually starts with one question: how many megawatts can be energized on this parcel, and when? Developers screen for proximity to existing high-voltage transmission, because tapping a 138 kV or 230 kV line nearby is usually far faster than waiting on new lines that require rights-of-way and easements.117 A parcel with a utility letter of authorization or a signed interconnection agreement carries a premium over unentitled land because it has retired what is often the largest schedule risk in the project.3

That is why "powered land" exists as a product. A developer assembles the acreage, secures zoning, negotiates the interconnection, and sometimes builds the substation, then sells or ground-leases the site to someone who will build the rest. The developer's value is almost entirely in the power position and the entitlements.

When the grid cannot deliver on time

Where utility timelines slip past contractual delivery dates, developers increasingly bring their own power: bridging generation on site, behind-the-meter gas plants, or campus designs that sit next to dedicated generation. CBRE's H1 2026 report describes bridge power gaining traction nationwide and points to Alberta, where the deregulated market lets operators contract power directly or build their own gas-fired supply.1 Owning generation moves the project from a real estate problem to a utility problem, with its own permitting, fuel and emissions exposure.

Buying the power, not just the connection

An interconnection decides whether power arrives. The procurement contract decides what it costs and who carries the price. The default is the utility tariff. Above it sit physical power purchase agreements, which deliver energy through the grid or from generation co-located with the campus, and virtual PPAs, financial contracts that settle against market prices and transfer clean-energy attributes while the site keeps buying its electricity from the utility.i The nuclear deals show the range. Microsoft signed a 20-year agreement supporting the restart of Three Mile Island Unit 1, now the Crane Clean Energy Center; Meta's 20-year agreement for the Clinton plant buys its clean-energy attributes rather than the electricity itself; and Talen agreed to supply Amazon up to 1,920 MW from Susquehanna.59 That Amazon supply now runs as a front-of-the-meter arrangement,60 after FERC declined in November 2024 to approve a larger co-located draw from the plant.61

05 · Regulation, by type and by state

In 2026 the rules started deciding the schedule

Most regulation lands at levels 1 and 2 of the stack, but it changes who pays at every level above them.

For a decade, state policy toward data centers meant incentives: sales and use tax exemptions on equipment and electricity, property tax abatements, and fast permitting. By 2026 that settlement had broken. NCSL counts 38 states with dedicated data center incentives. Lawmakers in at least 28 of them introduced bills this year to amend or curb their programs, and at least nine considered outright repeal.41 In parallel, utility commissions have written large-load tariffs that make data centers carry the cost and risk of the grid built to serve them, and regulators have started pausing projects outright. The instrument has shifted from a subsidy to a risk transfer.

The federal frame

On 18 June 2026 FERC issued show-cause orders to six grid operators: CAISO, ISO New England, MISO, NYISO, PJM and SPP. The orders grew out of the Energy Department's October 2025 advance notice of proposed rulemaking on large loads71 and work from a threshold of roughly 50 MW connecting at 69 kV or above.7374 They found the operators' large-load interconnection rules apparently inadequate and directed each to address five issues:21

  • study processes, including alternative transmission technologies;
  • preventing cost shifts and making transmission costs transparent;
  • co-location and behind-the-meter generation;
  • new services for flexible large loads;
  • studies for generation serving nearby loads.

FERC stated that states keep authority over retail terms, over who may sell power at retail, and over siting.21 Co-location in PJM runs on its own track: a December 2025 order directed PJM to offer new transmission services for loads sited at generators, after FERC had in November 2024 rejected an expanded Amazon draw from the Susquehanna nuclear plant.61

Federal tax and trade

The state map leaves out the federal levers, which move after-tax returns more than most state exemptions do. Three matter.i

Depreciation

100% bonus depreciation, made permanent

The July 2025 budget law (OBBBA) permanently restored 100% first-year expensing for qualifying property acquired after 19 January 2025. It applies to property with recovery periods of 20 years or less, so cost segregation studies that carve 5-, 7- and 15-year components out of a building decide how much of a facility can be expensed at once.83 The shell stays on its 39-year schedule; GPUs, servers and much of the electrical and mechanical plant do not.i

REITs

Why listed vehicles stop at level 7

A REIT must hold mostly real estate assets and earn mostly rents from real property. The 2016 final regulations use a data center as a worked example: central HVAC, security, fire suppression and humidity control are safe-harbor structural components, and the electrical and telecommunications systems qualify on the facts.84 Servers do not. That is why the REITs and listed trusts in the Capital table fund levels 3 to 7 and leave level 8 to someone else.i

Tariffs

Duties on the plant, not just the chips

Since 6 April 2026, Section 232 duties reach 50% on goods made almost entirely of steel, aluminum or copper and 25% on derivatives substantially made of them.85 Transformers were already supply-constrained by a shortage of electrical-grade steel;86 the duties land on the same equipment.i A separate semiconductor action left broader chip levies open.87

08 · Levels 4 and 5, as one system

Two paths through the building: power in, heat out

Every watt delivered to a GPU leaves the building as heat, so the electrical and mechanical plants are sized as a pair.

Lead times: single-source industry benchmarks compiled by Axis Intelligence Research, July 2026.5 single source Rack density: CBRE H1 2026.1

The electrical plant

Utility power arrives at high voltage and is stepped down at an on-site substation, then distributed at medium voltage through switchgear that also protects the building from faults. Closer to the halls it is stepped down again and routed through switchboards, overhead busway and power distribution units to the racks. In the middle sits the uninterruptible power supply: batteries (or flywheels) that hold the load for the seconds it takes generators to start and synchronize. Generators and their fuel carry the building through longer outages.

Redundancy is expressed in N notation. N is exactly enough equipment to carry the load; N+1 adds one spare unit; 2N duplicates the whole path so either side can carry everything. Enterprise and retail colocation buyers commonly pay for 2N or close to it. Some AI training designs accept less redundancy on the GPU load,i because a training run can checkpoint and restart, while a bank's transaction system cannot. That choice changes the cost per megawatt materially and is one of the first things to check when two quotes disagree.

Rack power is also changing form. NVIDIA has described an 800 V DC distribution scheme for its Kyber racks (the rack design planned for Vera Rubin Ultra), which would shift conversion equipment out of the rack and into the facility.i Treat the timing as vendor roadmap rather than committed fact, but it signals that electrical rooms designed today may be re-cut within one hardware generation.

The plant is bought before the building

Long-lead electrical equipment is now ordered before a project has a tenant. Developers place deposits of 20 to 30 percent on transformer orders four to five years before operations, under frame agreements whose escalation clauses track copper and steel indices; the developer takes the commodity risk in exchange for a production slot.92 Utilities describe buying for projects five years out and asking customers to prepay for long-lead equipment.93 The largest units, above 300 MVA, run 36 to 48 months,92 well beyond the 18 to 22 months cited above for typical switchgear and transformers.5 Until a lease is signed, the deposit works as the developer's option premium, forfeited if the project slips or dies.i

The mechanical plant

Air cooling works by pushing cold air through racks and pulling hot air out, with hot-aisle or cold-aisle containment keeping the two streams apart and computer-room air handlers moving heat into a water loop. Its practical ceiling depends on containment, supply-air temperature and rear-door assist, but benchmark compilations treat halls at roughly 40 to 80 kW per rack as liquid-cooled territory.5 CBRE puts conventional CPU servers at 3 to 10 kW per rack and describes 100 kW GPU servers as closed-loop liquid designs.1

Liquid cooling comes in three main forms. Rear-door heat exchangers bolt a coil onto the back of the rack and are the gentlest retrofit. Direct-to-chip cooling runs coolant through cold plates on the processors, with a coolant distribution unit (CDU) separating that rack-side loop from facility water; this is the standard approach for rack-scale Grace Blackwell (GB200, GB300) systems, while some eight-GPU Blackwell (B200, B300) servers still ship in air-cooled versions.i Immersion submerges whole servers in dielectric fluid and has so far seen narrower adoption.i At the far end, heat is rejected to the atmosphere through chillers, dry coolers or evaporative towers, which trade electricity against water consumption. The water side is measured as water usage effectiveness (WUE), liters consumed per kilowatt-hour of IT energy, and water use is now something several states require operators to report (see Rules).i

09 · Levels 3, 6 and 7

Shell, white space and the meet-me room

The parts a visitor sees are the cheapest to build and the slowest to change.

Industry vocabulary splits a facility into gray space and white space. Gray space holds the electrical and mechanical plant: switchgear rooms, UPS and battery rooms, generator yards, chiller plants. White space is the hall where racks stand. Older facilities put racks on raised floors with cold air in the plenum below; dense AI halls are generally built slab-on-grade with power and liquid piping run overhead, partly because fully loaded rack-scale GPU systems can exceed a tonne.i

"Fit-out" is the work of turning an empty, powered hall into rack positions: busway, containment, rack PDUs and, for liquid designs, CDUs and secondary piping. JLL notes tenant AI technology fit-out can run as much as $25 million per megawatt,7 which is why who pays for fit-out is often the largest single economic term in a lease. Many large tenants specify racks to Open Compute Project designs (open source hardware specifications), which standardizes the interface between building and IT.

The network enters through diverse fiber paths into a meet-me room, where carriers and cloud on-ramps interconnect with tenants by cross-connect. For retail colocation the density of carriers in that room is the product. For remote AI training campuses the question is simpler and harder: whether long-haul fiber reaches the site at all. CBRE counts more than 20,000 route miles of new fiber announced in 2026, much of it built to link new AI clusters back to core network hubs.1

10 · Levels 8 to 10

In AI builds, the computer usually costs more than the building

Adding the GPU stack raises all-in cost per megawatt again,56 and shortens the life of the capital at risk from decades to years.

Inside the racks sit accelerators, host CPUs, memory, local and shared storage, and two networks: a back-end fabric (InfiniBand or high-performance Ethernet) that ties GPUs into a single training or inference cluster, and a front-end network that connects the cluster to users, data and the outside world. Optics and switching have become a meaningful share of the bill as clusters grow. Storage is a modest share of cluster capex: one review of 2025 and 2026 bills of materials puts dedicated storage at up to about 15 percent for data-heavy training clusters and near zero for inference clusters that rely on drives inside each server.155 How much it changes the time accelerators spend waiting for data is the author's reading, not a sourced figure.i

Above the hardware sit operations and software. A facility operator keeps power and cooling running. A cloud operator adds provisioning, scheduling, multi-tenant isolation, monitoring and billing, and signs an SLA with the end customer. A full-stack AI provider goes one step further and sells a result: a managed training run, an inference endpoint, or tokens.

Training and inference want different buildings

Iron Mountain and Structure Research expect inference capacity to overtake training capacity in 2026 and to account for 80 percent of AI critical IT load by 2030. The two workloads site differently: training clusters can sit on large remote campuses chosen for land and power, while inference that serves users wants to be nearer population centers for latency and traffic.94 Operators describe the result as two infrastructure categories, with inference pulling some capacity back toward carrier-dense metro facilities.95

Most of the confusion in public discussion of data center costs comes from mixing these scopes. Shell and core, the scope most benchmarks measure, covers structure plus base power and cooling distribution. Tenant fit-out, IT hardware, land and interconnection sit outside it. When one source quotes $11 million per megawatt and another quotes three or four times that for the same project, both can be right.

Two further conventions trip readers. First, "per MW" usually means per megawatt of critical IT load, not total facility draw; with a power usage effectiveness (PUE) of 1.2 the utility connection is 20 percent larger. Second, redundancy: a 2N facility carries twice the electrical plant of an N design for the same IT load.

What the market pays over cost

A stabilized, leased Northern Virginia asset traded at roughly $27 million per MW in 2026 against a replacement cost of $11 to $14 million per MW, according to Foretel's compiled transaction data.16 The premium over replacement cost is the price of energized power and a signed tenant, the two things a greenfield builder cannot buy quickly. Digital Realty's 2026 purchase of Blackstone's interest in three assets, 288 MW stabilizing at $7.8 billion gross asset value, implies about $27 million per MW, the same level.1

Cushman & Wakefield reports development costs up 21 percent per MW since its prior guide.3 Turner & Townsend found 60 percent of surveyed professionals expect 5 to 15 percent construction inflation in 2026 and another 21 percent expect more.5 Any model anchored on a 2025 $/MW figure is already stale. Retail colocation

Sells: cabinets, cages and, above all, interconnection to the carriers and clouds in the building.

Paid by: per cabinet per month plus metered power and recurring cross-connect fees; Coloprice puts a North American 3 to 5 kW rack at $500 to $1,500 a month before power.15

Risk kept: many small tenants; the ecosystem is the moat and the cost.

Compute

GPU cloud (neocloud)

Sells: accelerator capacity as bare metal, virtual machines or managed clusters, often in leased wholesale halls.

Paid by: GPU-hour on demand, or reserved capacity on multi-year take-or-pay contracts. Coloprice tracked on-demand H100 pricing from $1.73 to $12.29 per GPU-hour, median $3.39, in September 2026.15

Risk kept: hardware depreciation, utilization and customer credit, financed with debt secured on the GPUs and the contracts.

Compute

Full-stack AI

Sells: outcomes: managed training, inference endpoints, tokens, or a dedicated "AI factory" operated end to end for one customer.

Paid by: per token, per request, per managed cluster, or a fixed service fee.

Risk kept: everything from the site to the model-serving software, plus pricing risk as per-token prices fall.

Ownership and capital overlays

The next six are not further points on the spectrum. They describe who owns or funds a delivery model, and each can sit on top of more than one: a powered shell can be sold and leased back, a build-to-suit campus can be held in a joint venture, and a GPU cloud's capacity can be pledged against a contract with a hyperscaler. One campus can therefore carry a delivery model and two or three overlays at once.

Overlay

Sale-leaseback

What it is: a change of owner, not of product. An owner-operator sells a stabilized facility to an investor and leases it back, converting owned plant into lease cost and recycling the capital into new builds.

Paid by: purchase price at a cap rate set by tenant credit and lease term.

Risk kept: the buyer holds residual value; the seller keeps operational control.

Overlay

Hyperscale self-build

What it is: the occupier owns and funds every level of a build-to-suit or turnkey design for itself, then sells cloud services on top.

Paid by: balance sheet capex, which is exactly what the next structure is designed to move off balance sheet.

Risk kept: all of it, with the lowest cost of capital in the industry.

Overlay

JV or SPV with a short lease and a guarantee

What it is: third-party capital owns a campus the occupier builds and runs, typically on a build-to-suit design.

Paid by: lease payments from the hyperscaler to the vehicle, backed by a residual value guarantee.

Risk kept: by design, split. Hyperion is the template, described under Capital.

Overlay

Capacity contracts between providers

What it is: an overlay on a GPU cloud: blocks of capacity sold from a neocloud to a hyperscaler or model lab that needs it faster than it can build.

Paid by: multi-year committed contracts, which then serve as collateral for the neocloud's own GPU debt.

Risk kept: concentration; one customer contract can underwrite an entire financing vehicle.

Overlay

Hyperscaler lease backstop

What it is: a hyperscaler guarantees a GPU cloud's lease payments to the site owner, so the site's debt is underwritten partly to the hyperscaler's credit. Google has backstopped Fluidstack's leases at sites owned by TeraWulf, Cipher and Hut 8, about $5.8 billion in total.63

Paid by: equity in the site owner; at TeraWulf, warrants for about 14 percent of the company against a $3.2 billion backstop.68

Risk kept: the backstop takes effect only at lease commencement, and on default Google can pay the termination fee or step in as tenant.65 Construction and delivery risk before that date stays with the developer and its lenders.i

Overlay

Vendor commitments and GPU leases

What it is: the chip supplier or a purpose-built vehicle carries part of level 8. NVIDIA signed a $6.3 billion order to buy CoreWeave's unsold capacity through April 2032.69 In a reported xAI structure, an SPV buys NVIDIA GPUs and rents them to xAI for five years, with NVIDIA among the equity investors.70

Paid by: capacity payments or lease rentals; in the SPV, lenders look to the GPUs and the rental stream rather than the user's balance sheet.70

Risk kept: supplier, financier and customer sit in one counterparty chain, so a single demand shock reaches all three.i

Hyperion: moving a campus off the balance sheet

Meta and funds managed by Blue Owl formed a joint venture in October 2025 to develop and own Hyperion, with Blue Owl funds holding 80 percent and Meta 20 percent, and the partners funding their shares of roughly $27 billion in development costs. Meta manages construction and operations, and leases the whole campus from the venture on operating leases with a four-year initial term and extension options. Four years is short for a hyperscale lease, which is where the key instrument comes in: Meta gave the venture a residual value guarantee for the first 16 years, a capped payment tied to the campus's value if Meta does not renew and certain conditions are met. Part of Blue Owl's capital came from debt placed with PIMCO and other bond investors.9

That debt is the largest private bond deal on record. Beignet Investor, a Blue Owl vehicle, priced $27.3 billion of 6.581 percent senior secured notes due May 2049 in October 2025.54 S&P rated them A+, one notch below Meta, on the view that the contract passes substantial credit risk to Meta through construction and operation.5456 PIMCO took about $18 billion and BlackRock funds more than $3 billion;5756 Blue Owl put in about $7 billion of cash and Meta took a $3 billion distribution at closing.56 The campus is a 2.1 GW facility slated for completion in June 2029.54 In 2026 the notes traded as low as about 94 cents on the dollar on a day Meta's stock rose 9 percent:57 bondholders price the guarantee's tail separately from the tenant.

The design gives each side something it needs. As analysts have read the structure, Meta is not expected to consolidate the campus or its debt,1810 and it keeps the option to walk away every few years. Bondholders get credit support anchored to Meta through the lease and the guarantee. The residual value guarantee is the whole deal in miniature: it is a price on the risk that a purpose-built AI campus is worth less than its cost once its first tenant leaves.

The accounting that decides "off balance sheet"

Two standards do the work. ASC 842 classifies the lease: an operating lease puts a right-of-use asset and a lease liability on the lessee's balance sheet but keeps the campus's cost and debt off it, and any amount the lessee expects to owe under a residual value guarantee counts toward the lease payments.i ASC 810 decides who consolidates the venture: under its power-plus-economics test for variable interest entities, Hyperion is designed to point at Blue Owl rather than Meta.55 Critics read the result as Meta credit in project-finance form;5518 S&P's view is that Meta's rating would be unchanged even with the campus consolidated.55

Depreciation is the other accounting choice that moves reported numbers. Hyperscalers book servers over five to six years: Meta extended most servers to 5.5 years in 2025, Alphabet and Microsoft use six, and Amazon cut a subset from six to five.58 A longer book life lowers reported depreciation; it does not lengthen the economic life lenders underwrite.i

When the money runs in a circle

Some of the largest commitments are circular. NVIDIA agreed to invest up to $100 billion in OpenAI as OpenAI deploys NVIDIA systems; AMD gave OpenAI warrants for up to 10 percent of its shares, vesting as OpenAI deploys 6 GW of AMD chips.196 OpenAI's roughly $300 billion contract with Oracle pays for capacity that Oracle fills with NVIDIA chips.182179 Each link is a sale to someone, but the cash at the start of the chain comes from a supplier's balance sheet and an AI lab's ability to keep raising money.i A demand shock reaches every party at once, which is why lenders in this note keep asking whose credit sits at the end of the chain.

The pattern

Whoever builds the longest-lived asset asks for the longest contract from the strongest name.

Utilities and energy companies want 10 to 20 years of take-or-pay from a hyperscaler before they build a plant. Building owners want 15-year leases or a residual guarantee. GPU lenders accept three to five years, because the chips will not last longer. The gaps between those clocks are where every structure in this table earns its fee.i

17 · Where the risk sits

Every structure is an answer to the same question

The point

Who owns the asset when the tenant's needs change faster than the building does?

Powered shells leave obsolescence with the tenant. Turnkey colocation keeps it with the landlord. Hyperion splits it through a guarantee. GPU debt prices it into the spread. A hyperscaler backstop lends a stronger balance sheet to a weaker tenant, once the building is delivered. Reading any deal starts with finding where that risk has been put.

Eight risks recur across the structures, and each maps to levels of the stack.

Duration mismatch (levels 3 to 8). Buildings, leases, GPU loans and customer contracts run on different clocks. A long powered shell lease to an investment-grade hyperscaler behaves much like a real estate bond;i a three-year GPU contract financed with five-year debt is partly a bet on residual compute prices.13

Tenant credit (all levels). CBRE reports owners becoming more selective as portfolios skew toward AI tenants, and flags that emerging-market demand comes largely from AI training companies with short operating histories.1 The spread between DDTL 4.0 and DDTL 5.5 reflects that risk, together with contract length.1113 Trace the chain: a campus leased to a GPU cloud that serves a model lab carries the lab's credit unless a backstop, a vendor commitment or a parent guarantee substitutes a stronger name at one link.i

Density obsolescence (levels 4 to 6). An air-cooled hall can be stranded by one hardware generation. CBRE notes that converting air-cooled space to liquid can push ready-for-service dates out beyond six months, and that operators often end up concentrating power into a few dense racks while floor space goes unused.1

Energization (level 2). Record preleasing (80.4 percent of capacity under construction was committed in H1 20261) means tenants have signed for power that does not exist yet. Delivery dates, delay penalties and bridge-power obligations decide who absorbs a slipped interconnect.

Policy and community (levels 1 and 2). Large-load tariffs, consumption taxes and local opposition are now priced into site selection rather than treated as tail risks. State pauses in 2026 added a risk that cannot be priced at all; see Rules.

Workload fit (levels 1 to 8). A campus built for training is sited, powered and made redundant for one kind of tenant. If inference reaches 80 percent of AI load by 2030, as one forecast has it,94 remote training campuses face a thinner pool of second users than metro halls.i

Equipment and delivery (levels 4 to 7). Transformer and switchgear deposits are committed years before a lease,92 while credit support such as a hyperscaler backstop starts only at commencement.65 The window between the deposit and the commencement date belongs to the developer alone.i

Insurance capacity (levels 3 to 8). S&P Global Ratings estimates total insurable values of $20 to $30 billion for a single hyperscale campus, against limits of $5 to $10 billion for the largest traditional infrastructure projects.96 Insurers place limits against probable maximum loss rather than full value, which can leave as much as half of a campus's cost uninsured,97 and Zurich reports weather as the leading source of loss in its data center builders' risk book.98 The contract then has to say who carries the uninsured slice.i

Terms shifting toward landlords

CBRE describes two changes in 2026 lease terms. "Take or pay" floors, requiring tenants to pay for 60 to 85 percent of reserved power whether or not they use it, are becoming common in the US. And colocation operators are starting to adopt triple-net structures in which tenants assume taxes, maintenance and insurance. Both move risk down the table toward the tenant while vacancy sits at a record 1.4 percent; expect them to be renegotiated if supply catches up.1

18 · Reading a deal

Twenty-three questions for any data center transaction

Most disagreements about a project's value dissolve once these are answered on the same basis.

SCOPE
 1. Which levels does the $/MW figure include: shell only, fit-out, IT?
 2. Is "MW" critical IT load or utility connection? What PUE and WUE are assumed?
 3. What redundancy (N, N+1, 2N) and what rack density is it designed for?
 4. Training or inference: what workload is it built for, and who else could use it?

POWER
 5. Is the interconnect signed, or a letter? Who built and owns the substation?
 6. Who pays if energization slips past the contractual delivery date?
 7. Who holds the utility account and absorbs large-load tariffs?
 8. How is energy bought (tariff, physical PPA, virtual PPA), and who carries the price?
 9. If the grid date slips, what on-site generation is planned, and who carries
    fuel price and air permits?
10. If a utility or energy company built generation for this campus, how long
    does the customer pay for it, and who carries the plant afterward?

PROCUREMENT AND DELIVERY
11. Which long-lead items hold factory slots or deposits, who paid, and what is
    forfeited if the project slips?
12. What insurance limits are placed against total insurable value, and who
    carries the gap?

LEASE
13. Term vs asset life vs debt maturity: where are the gaps, and who holds each?
14. Take-or-pay floor? Pass-through or triple-net? Escalators?
15. Who funds fit-out, and who owns it at lease end?

CREDIT AND EXIT
16. Tenant credit rating, and what backs it (parent guarantee, RVG, hyperscaler
    backstop, vendor commitment, contracts)?
17. When does that credit support start, and what voids it?
18. Can the halls be re-let to a different density or a different tenant type?
19. What does the asset trade for stabilized vs its replacement cost, and what
    yield on cost does the rent support?
20. What is the takeout: securitization, sale or recapitalization, and on what
    stabilized NOI?

RULES AND TAX
21. Which incentives does the model assume, who claims them, and are they paused,
    capped, time-limited or subject to clawback?
22. Which large-load tariff applies (minimum demand, term, collateral, exit fee),
    and what is pending (FERC dockets, state audits, moratoria)?
23. Which federal assumptions are built in: bonus depreciation, REIT eligibility,
    tariff exposure on plant and IT?
19 · Reference

Glossary

Electrical and redundancy

Critical IT load. Power available to the servers themselves, the unit most leases and cost benchmarks use.

PUE (power usage effectiveness). Total facility power divided by IT power. 1.0 would mean zero overhead for cooling and conversion.

N, N+1, 2N. Exactly enough equipment; one spare; a fully duplicated path.

MV / LV. Medium voltage (distribution around the campus) and low voltage (the final feed to racks).

UPS. Uninterruptible power supply: batteries or flywheels that carry the load until generators take over.

EPMS, BMS, DCIM. Electrical power monitoring, building management and data center infrastructure management software.

Behind-the-meter (BTM). Generation on the customer side of the utility meter, supplying the site directly.

Cooling and the hall

CRAH / CRAC. Computer-room air handler (chilled water) or air conditioner (refrigerant).

RDHx. Rear-door heat exchanger, a liquid coil mounted on the back of a rack.

Direct-to-chip (DLC). Coolant through cold plates mounted on processors.

CDU. Coolant distribution unit, the heat exchanger and pumps that separate the rack-side loop from facility water.

Gray space / white space. Plant rooms versus the hall where racks stand.

MMR. Meet-me room, where carriers and tenants interconnect.

RFS. Ready for service, the date a tenant can install and energize equipment.

WUE (water usage effectiveness). Liters of water consumed per kilowatt-hour of IT energy. Lower is better.

Leases and finance

Powered land / powered shell / warm shell. Site with a power position; building with power delivered; building with part of the plant installed.

Turnkey. Space delivered fully conditioned, ready for racks.

NNN (triple-net). Tenant pays taxes, insurance and maintenance on top of rent.

Modified gross plus electricity. The traditional colocation structure: rent includes most operating costs, power is passed through.

Take or pay. Minimum payment on reserved capacity regardless of usage.

RVG. Residual value guarantee, a promise to cover a shortfall in asset value at lease end.

SASB CMBS. Single-asset, single-borrower commercial mortgage-backed securities.

DDTL. Delayed-draw term loan, drawn in tranches as equipment is delivered.

Yield on cost. Stabilized net operating income divided by total development cost, the developer's core return metric.

Cap rate. Stabilized net operating income divided by price; the yield a buyer accepts.

Development spread. Yield on cost minus the market cap rate, the margin that rewards building rather than buying.

Backstop / recognition agreement. A third party's commitment to pay a tenant's lease obligations, documented in an agreement among landlord, tenant and the backstop provider.

Bonus depreciation / cost segregation. Immediate expensing of qualifying short-lived property; the engineering study that separates such property from the building.

Book (useful) life. The period over which an asset is depreciated in the accounts, which need not match its economic life.

ASC 842. The lease accounting standard. An operating lease records a right-of-use asset and lease liability but keeps the underlying asset and its debt off the lessee's balance sheet.

VIE / ASC 810. Variable interest entity; the consolidation test that asks who has power over the entity and exposure to its economics.

Power procurement and rules

PPA (power purchase agreement). A long-term contract to buy electricity, physically delivered or financially settled.

Virtual PPA. A financially settled PPA: the buyer receives the project's clean-energy attributes and settles price differences while still buying its actual power from the utility.

Front-of-meter / co-location. Supply delivered through the grid; a load sited at a generator, which FERC now treats as its own category in PJM.

Large load. In FERC's 2026 orders, roughly 50 MW or more connecting at 69 kV or above. States and utilities set their own thresholds, from 25 MW to 100 MW in the tables above.

ANOPR. Advance notice of proposed rulemaking. DOE's October 2025 notice started the federal large-load process.

Show-cause order (FPA Section 206). A FERC order requiring a grid operator to justify its tariff or change it.

Abeyance. A pause in a FERC proceeding, granted here so PJM and MISO could file reforms later.

PILOT. Payment in lieu of taxes, a negotiated payment that replaces some or all of a property tax bill.

Present value. Future amounts discounted to today at a chosen rate, so a break worth $10M in year ten counts for less than $10M now.

Business personal property (TPP). Movable business assets such as servers and equipment, which most states tax and some exempt entirely.

Host community agreement. A side agreement with a town, county or school district for payments or commitments beyond taxes.

Funding, exits and power on site

ABS (asset-backed securities). Bonds repaid from a pool of leases or contracts; in data centers, usually stabilized, leased campuses.

CMBS / SASB. Commercial mortgage-backed securities; single-asset, single-borrower deals finance one large property or campus.

Mini-perm. A construction loan that runs a few years past completion, bridging to permanent financing.

Take-private. Purchase of a listed company by private owners, ending its listing.

Platform sale. Sale of a whole operating company, with its pipeline and team, rather than single buildings.

Recapitalization. Selling a stake or replacing debt to return capital without a full exit.

Carrier hotel. A building where many networks terminate and exchange traffic; the origin of retail colocation.

Cross-connect. A physical cable between two customers in the same building, billed monthly.

Wholesale and retail colocation. Whole halls leased per kW on long terms, versus cabinets and cages sold with interconnection.

Time to power. The span from an interconnection request to the date a utility can deliver contracted megawatts.

LCOE. Levelized cost of energy: capital, fuel and operating cost spread over the energy produced.

Heat rate. Fuel burned per kWh generated, in Btu; lower is more efficient.

Slot reservation. A paid place in a manufacturer's production schedule, before a firm order.

kWh excise. A tax levied per kilowatt-hour rather than as a percentage of the bill, as in Ohio and Virginia.

Minimum bill. A tariff or contract term that charges a set share of contracted demand whether or not the power is used.

Take-or-pay. A contract under which the buyer pays for committed capacity whether or not it takes delivery.

Clean transition tariff. A utility rate through which one customer pays the premium for a new clean, firm resource, as in Nevada.

Industrial revenue bond / Chapter 100 / bond-for-title. Taxable bonds issued by a local authority that hold title to a project so it escapes property tax, in exchange for payments in lieu of taxes; the developer usually buys the bonds itself.

Transferable tax credit. A federal energy credit sold for cash to a corporate buyer, used to fund construction.

Capital recycling. Selling stakes in finished assets into funds or joint ventures and reinvesting the proceeds in new development.

Warrant. A right to buy a company's shares at a set price, used here by chipmakers to pay customers in equity.
